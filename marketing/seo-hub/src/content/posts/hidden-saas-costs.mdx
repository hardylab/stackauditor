# Hidden SaaS costs: 7 things you're paying for but not using

Your SaaS bill is bigger than it should be. Not by 5%. By an average of 30%, according to the 2025 Zylo SaaS management benchmark. The waste is not in the tools you use — it's in the tools you stopped using, the seats you forgot about, and the features you never opened.

This page is for solo founders, fractional operators, and 1–5 person agencies who already suspect they are over-paying but do not have time for a half-day audit. It is the short version — seven specific places to look, what to look for in each, and a 10-minute script to find the worst offenders. The full audit lives in [How to audit your SaaS stack in one afternoon](/marketing/seo-hub/p/saas-spend-audit/index.html); this is the triage list you run before the audit.

By the end you will have a number — your annualized wasted spend, in dollars — and a short list of tools to cut, renegotiate, or migrate. The number is the only thing that matters.

## What "hidden" actually means

There are three categories of hidden SaaS cost, and most operators only know about one of them.

### The known waste

Tools you know you should cancel but have not gotten around to. The annual plan that auto-renews in 30 days. The free trial that became a paid line item six months ago. This is the lowest-hanging fruit and is usually the smallest dollar amount — once you notice it, you cancel it within a quarter.

### The dormant waste

Seats that exist but are unused. A contractor left and the seat was never removed. A team member switched tools and the old license kept billing. A seat set up "just in case" for a hire that fell through. Dormant seats are the largest line item for solo operators and the hardest to spot — they do not show up in your inbox, they show up in someone else's admin panel.

### The duplicate waste

Two tools doing one job. Two CRMs because one came free with a bundle. Two scheduling tools because nobody picked the canonical one. Two password managers because the last hire had a preference. Duplicate tools are the most expensive per line item because you are paying for the full subscription on both — but they are also the easiest to detect once you look at the full list.

## The 7 things to look for

These are ranked by typical dollar value for a solo operator running 15–25 SaaS tools. The top three are 70–80% of the waste by dollar.

### 1. Dormant seats (the biggest line item)

A "dormant seat" is any seat that has not logged in for 60+ days. Most tools bill per seat per month whether the seat is used or not, so a dormant seat is 100% waste.

Where to look: Slack admin → People → "Last active". Google Workspace admin → Users → "Last sign-in". Notion workspace settings → Members → "Last edited". Stripe dashboard → Team → "Last active". Any tool that charges per seat has the same data, usually under "Settings" → "Team" or "Members".

The test: any seat with last-active > 60 days. If the seat is on an annual plan, the dormant seat hurts you until renewal. If monthly, it hurts you until the next bill.

### 2. Unused feature tiers

Most SaaS products have 3–5 pricing tiers, and the most expensive tier is usually 3–5x the cheapest. Operators tend to start at the cheap tier, hit a wall, upgrade to the next tier, then forget which features triggered the upgrade. Six months later they are paying for the tier-3 features and using the tier-2 ones.

Where to look: the tool's pricing page side-by-side with your current usage. The honest test: which tier-X features did your team actually open in the last 30 days? If you cannot name three, you can probably drop a tier.

This is the trickiest line item to evaluate because downgrading a tier is irreversible — you lose access to features that someone, somewhere, was using. The right move is usually a 14-day test at the lower tier before you commit.

### 3. Duplicate tools in the same category

The single most common category of duplicate is project management — solo operators average 2.4 PM tools. CRM is the second (1.8 average). Scheduling tools, password managers, note-taking apps, and time trackers all show similar patterns.

Where to look: list every tool you pay for, group by category, count by group. Any category with 2+ tools is a candidate. The honest test: does the team genuinely use both, or did the second one become the default and the first one a fallback?

The right move is almost always to keep the one the team uses most, even if the other one has more features. Feature count and team fit are different things.

### 4. Annual plans that should be monthly

Annual plans save 15–25% over monthly, which is a real discount — if you will use the tool for the full year. If you are not sure you will, the discount is a trap. The trap looks like: "we will save $200 over the year", and then in month 4 you stop using it, and now you have 8 months of prepaid waste.

Where to look: any tool on annual contract where you cannot confidently say you will use it for the full year. If the renewal is more than 6 months away and the usage is dropping, switch to monthly now — even at the higher rate, you come out ahead if you cancel in 3 months.

### 5. Free trials that converted silently

Every SaaS free trial that converts silently is a tool you are paying for that you did not consciously choose to keep. Most operators notice these within a quarter — but some do not, and the cumulative cost across 4–6 of them is meaningful.

Where to look: scan your last 90 days of card and ACH transactions for any SaaS line item you cannot recall signing up for in the last 30 days. The match: charges you did not actively decide to start paying for.

### 6. Personal cards being expensed silently

Solo operators buy a lot of tools on personal cards and expense them. The expense flow is fine for tax purposes; the problem is that personal-card purchases do not show up in your "tools" budget line the way business-card purchases do, so the cost is invisible to the quarterly review.

Where to look: your last 90 days of personal-card transactions, filtered for any recurring charge > $10/mo. The fix is not to stop expensing — it is to put the recurring tools on a business card so they show up in the right budget line.

### 7. "Just in case" tools

These are tools you bought for a project that did not happen, or a need that did not materialize. They are usually low-dollar but high-count. The cost is not the subscription — it is the cognitive cost of having one more tool you have to remember exists.

Where to look: list every tool you have not opened in 30+ days. If you cannot name a specific reason you will open it in the next 30, cancel it.

## The 10-minute audit script

You do not need a half-day to find the worst offenders. This script runs in 10 minutes and surfaces 70–80% of the dollar value of waste.

**Step 1 (3 min):** Export the last 90 days of transactions from your business card and ACH history. Tag every line item as a SaaS charge. The list will be longer than you expect.

**Step 2 (2 min):** Open each tool's admin panel. Find the "active users last 90 days" or "last active" column. Sort tools by annual cost descending. The top 5 tools are 70–80% of your wasted spend.

**Step 3 (2 min):** For each of the top 5 tools, count dormant seats (last-active > 60 days). Multiply by per-seat annual cost. That is your "dormant waste" number.

**Step 4 (2 min):** Group the rest of the tools by category. Any category with 2+ tools is a duplicate candidate. Pick the keep-list and the cut-list.

**Step 5 (1 min):** Add up the annualized cost of every tool you flagged as cut. That number is your ROI.

## FAQ

### What is the average hidden SaaS cost for a small business?

The Zylo 2025 SaaS Management Index puts waste at 30%+ of total SaaS spend for organizations of every size. For solo operators and 1–5 person agencies, the number is closer to 25–35% because there are fewer overlapping seats but more single-owner tools that drift. A solo operator spending $500/mo on SaaS realistically wastes $125–$175/mo.

### How do I find dormant SaaS seats?

Open every tool's admin panel. Most tools (Slack, Google Workspace, Notion, Stripe, Figma, Linear, ClickUp, Asana, GitHub, GitLab, HubSpot, Salesforce) have a "last active" or "last login" column under Team / Members / People settings. Flag any seat with last-active > 60 days. The whole exercise is 5–10 minutes for a 15-tool stack.

### How do I know if I'm paying for an unused tier?

Open the tool's pricing page side-by-side with your current usage. Look at the features listed under your tier. Open each of those features in the tool — if you have not used a feature in the last 30 days, you are paying for it without using it. The honest test: name three features your tier unlocks that you actually use. If you cannot, downgrade.

### Are duplicate SaaS tools actually a problem?

Yes, especially for project management, CRM, scheduling, note-taking, and password managers. Most solo operators run 2+ tools in at least one of these categories. The cost is the full subscription on each. The fix is to pick one canonical tool per category and cancel the rest — even if the other one has more features, the team's actual workflow matters more than the feature count.

### Should I switch from annual to monthly SaaS plans?

Only if you cannot confidently say you will use the tool for the full annual term. Annual plans save 15–25%, which is real money if you keep the tool. If you are unsure, switch to monthly — the discount is not worth the prepaid waste if you cancel in month 4.
